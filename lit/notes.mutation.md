## NOVELTY CHECK (not killed)

I found **no** paper that measures the mutation-based oracle strength of **public Cucumber/Gherkin
(BDD) suites**, that restricts mutation to the **domain layer**, that **separates BDD-only from
full-suite kill**, or that tracks **erosion over time**. The nearest neighbour is **Mutta**
(Leotta/Paparella/Ricca, SQJ 2023), which does measure mutation score of **end-to-end web suites**
(>15k mutants, ~87k E2E test runs) and also **conditions on coverage** ("took into account the
coverage information to consider only the mutants actually executed"). Mutta is a *tool paper plus a
case study comparing assertion-based vs differential E2E testing* on two web applications; it is not
a corpus study of public BDD suites, has no BDD-only/full-suite separation, and no longitudinal
component. Our novelty therefore survives, but Mutta must be cited and explicitly differentiated.
Second-nearest: **Jain et al. 2023** deliberately **excludes** integration/system tests, which is the
gap we fill. Two targeted searches for `mutation testing BDD/Cucumber` returned no academic hits.

## Papers opened

### Just2014AreMutants — Are mutants a valid substitute for real faults in software testing? (FSE 2014)
- **What it does:** tests the central validity assumption of mutation analysis — that a suite's mutant-detection rate tracks its real-fault-detection rate — while explicitly controlling for code coverage.
- **Method / data:** 5 large open-source Java programs (321 KLOC total), **357 real, developer-fixed, manually isolated faults**, **230,000 mutants** (Major), 480 developer-written test-suite pairs ⟨T_pass, T_fail⟩ plus **35,141 automatically-generated** suites. Coverage measured with Cobertura; point-biserial and rank-biserial correlations.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - coupling exists for **73%** of real faults; **27%** of real faults are "not coupled to the mutants generated by commonly used mutation operators"; **17%** "are not coupled to any mutants" (mostly algorithmic changes / code deletion); **10%** "require a new or stronger mutation operator".
  - mutation score increased for **362 out of 480** pairs (75%) vs statement coverage for only **222 out of 480** (46%); for the **258** pairs where statement coverage did **not** increase, mutation score still increased in **153** (59%).
  - when coverage is controlled, on average **2 mutants** are coupled to a single real fault; COR, ROR and statement-deletion mutants are "more often coupled to real faults than other mutants".
  - the mutation-score↔fault-detection correlation is significantly stronger than the statement-coverage↔fault-detection correlation for all subject programs except Joda-Time (p > 0.2).
  - equivalent mutants: "We did not eliminate equivalent mutants, which means that our reported mutation scores might be underestimated. This is, however, not a concern for our study because we do not interpret absolute mutation scores."
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** nothing at suite level below/above unit granularity; all subjects are Java libraries with unit suites; no acceptance/BDD suites; no longitudinal axis.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none.

### Andrews2005IsMutation — Is mutation an appropriate tool for testing experiments? (ICSE 2005)
- **What it does:** asks whether mutation operators produce faults that are representative enough for controlled experiments, comparing mutants against real faults and hand-seeded faults.
- **Method / data:** "a number of programs with comprehensive pools of test cases and known faults" (abstract-level description only; full text not retrievable — see Dead ends).
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** NR (no quantitative value retrievable). Conclusion: "the use of mutation operators is yielding trustworthy results (generated mutants are similar to real faults). Mutants appear however to be different from hand-seeded faults that seem to be harder to detect than real faults."
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no numbers; no system-level suites; no equivalent-mutant protocol.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none. It is the historical anchor for "mutants ≈ real faults", which we need for the oracle-strength argument.

### Papadakis2019MutationAdvances — Mutation Testing Advances: An Analysis and Survey (Advances in Computers 112, 2019)
- **What it does:** the field's reference survey and, crucially for us, a "mini-handbook" of best practice for using mutation in controlled experiments (Section 9 + a seven-point checklist in Section 10).
- **Method / data:** narrative + systematic survey of ~2008–2017 literature (publication count grows ~linear, R²=0.887 in Fig. 1).
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - Seven-point checklist (verbatim headings): "1. Mutant Selection: Explain the choice of mutant operators." … "2. Mutation Testing Tool: Justify the choice of mutation testing tool." … "3. Mutant redundancy: Justify the steps taken to control mutant redundancy." … "4. Test suite choice and size" … "5. Clean Program Assumption" … "6. Multiple experimental repetitions" … "7. Presentation of the results: Clarify the granularity level of the empirical results."
  - "The choice of mutation testing tool needs to be made carefully as at the current state, mutation testing tools differ significantly"; "more than a quarter of the empirical studies does not report such details" (tool/operator-level details).
  - "Detecting equivalent mutants is a well-known undecidable problem"; best available route is heuristics, notably TCE (compiler optimisation).
  - reports that random mutant selection with **5%** of mutants predicts the mutation score "with high accuracy" (citing Zhang et al.); second-order strategies can remove **80% to 90%** of equivalent mutants at ≈10% or less effectiveness loss (citing Papadakis & Malevris).
  - Yao et al.'s recommendation to drop the ABS class and part of the UOI class to reduce equivalents and improve score accuracy.
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no BDD/acceptance-level guidance; granularity point (7) is raised but not resolved for system-level suites.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; it *defines* the reporting bar we must clear.

### Jain2023MindTheGap — Mind the Gap: The Difference Between Coverage and Mutation Score Can Guide Testing Efforts (ISSRE 2023; arXiv 2309.02395)
- **What it does:** defines the **oracle gap** = coverage − mutation score, i.e. exactly the "coverage asks whether code ran, mutation asks whether anyone checked" argument that motivates our coverage-conditioning.
- **Method / data:** observational study of **45** popular Maven Java projects, **12,051 files**, **463,417 LOC**, **44,320 mutants** (universalmutator, regex/AST source-level operators), plus a synthetic experiment on 25 well-tested files in 5 projects (mutation analysis cost: "20 CPU weeks over 19 AWS EC2 instances") and a blockchain case study (Bitcoin vs Algorand).
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - raw coverage↔mutation-score regression: correlation coefficient **0.7479**, r = **0.757**, R² = **0.573**; covered-only: **0.2066**, r = **0.145**, R² = **0.021**.
  - oracle-gap variance **603.92** (raw) and **1142.74** (covered); variance by coverage bucket 25.35 (0–25%) vs 823.31 (75–100%).
  - manual audit of the **26** files with >80% coverage and <20% mutation score: **12** had multiple missing asserts, **7** had one operator-specific missing assertion, **7** were false positives (message-string/log mutations).
  - sampling: "We therefore sampled up to 100 mutants per file, for up to 100 files per project"; median 236 mutants/file but median 30.5 sampled → **12.9%** sample rate.
  - equivalent mutants: "We performed no analysis to remove equivalent mutants, as our use of mutation scores is focused only on comparing differences across projects, where we can assume equivalence rates are sufficiently similar across projects to not substantially impact our results."
  - **excludes system/integration tests by design**: "We exclude integration or system tests in these experiments, and unit tests that check for exceptional behavior" (because assertions cannot be programmatically varied).
  - crypto example: Bitcoin 98.7% statement coverage / 75.85% mutation score vs Algorand 90% coverage / 99.7% mutation score.
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** unit-level Java only; explicitly refuses to handle system tests; no temporal dimension; no BDD.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none — and it *hands us* the exclusion that defines our contribution.

### Petrovic2021DoesMutationTesting — Does mutation testing improve testing practices? (ICSE 2021)
- **What it does:** industrial, longitudinal, interventional study of whether showing mutants to developers improves suites, plus a fault-coupling analysis against real high-priority bugs.
- **Method / data:** Google monorepo; **14,730,562 mutants** for **662,584** code changes in **446,604** files (six years); coupling analysis on **1,502** retained bugs, "almost 400 thousand mutants, and over 33 million test target executions"; 5 operators (AOR, LCR, ROR, UOI, SBR); ≤7 findings/file; 1 mutant per line.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - "We found that for 1043 (70%) of the bugs, mutation testing would have reported a fault-coupled mutant in the bug-introducing change."
  - **459 of 1502 (30%)** bugs not coupled to any generated mutant; manual sample of 50: weak operator **14/50**, missing operator **13/50**, "no such mutation operator" **23/50**.
  - test hunks per file vs exposure: Spearman **r_s = 0.9** (p<.001); coverage-only baseline **r_s = −0.24**; median changed test hunks 1 (mutation) vs 0 (coverage).
  - mutant survivability vs exposure **r_s = −0.50**; "more than 90% of all lines have a mutant majority fate of 100%".
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** unit-test granularity, Google's own code, no public corpus, no BDD.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none. It is our best defence that mutants stay coupled to real bugs in modern repositories.

### Petrovic2022PracticalMutationTesting — Practical Mutation Testing at Scale: A view from Google (IEEE TSE 2022)
- **What it does:** describes and validates the production mutation system; the canonical statement of what "practical mutation testing at scale" costs and what must be thrown away.
- **Method / data:** ~2 billion LOC; >500M test executions/day; 60,000 changes/day; **24,000+ developers**, **1,000+ projects**; evaluation on "almost 17 million mutants and 760,000 changes" surfacing 2 million mutants; 10 languages; 5 Mothra operators.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - "even when applying sampling techniques to substantially reduce the number of mutants, developers at Google initially classified **85%** of reported mutants as unproductive"; suppression heuristics moved the productive ratio "from 15% to 89%".
  - "we were also unable to find a good way to surface it [the mutation score] to the developers in an actionable way, as it is neither concrete nor actionable, and it does not guide testing."
  - live mutants per changelist: median **2**, 99th percentile **43**.
  - heuristic inventory (Table 2): Uncompilable 1, **Equivalent 13**, Unproductive-killable 16, Redundant 2.
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no notion of a project-level score (deliberately abandoned); nothing about acceptance-level suites.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none, but it is the strongest published critique of "mutation score as a number" and we must answer it (we do: our score is a *comparative* oracle-strength index, not a developer-facing goal).

### Papadakis2016ThreatsValidity — Threats to the validity of mutation-based test assessment (ISSTA 2016)
- **What it does:** shows that mutation-based comparisons are systematically invalid when mutant sets contain both subsuming and subsumed mutants.
- **Method / data:** 5 Unix utilities (Grep, Sed, Flex, Make, Gzip); repeated experiments (30 or 1,000 repetitions each); plus a survey of **91** papers from ISSTA/(ESEC)FSE/ICSE (25 use mutation, **17** are mutation-based assessments).
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - "Our findings indicate that Type I errors occur, for arbitrary experiments that fail to take countermeasures, approximately **62%** of the time."
  - "we found that **68%** are vulnerable to this threat to validity" (of the 17 mutation-based assessment papers; none considered mutant subsumption).
  - "fewer than **5%** of all mutants are subsuming (and not subsumed themselves). The remaining 95% of mutants are subsumed by some other mutants."
  - mutation used in "approximately 19% of the software testing studies" at those venues.
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** Java/C unit benchmarks; no equivalent-mutant protocol beyond noting TCE; nothing on suite granularity.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; it dictates that we control mutant redundancy or our cross-suite comparisons are void.

### Yao2014EquivalentStubborn — A study of equivalent and stubborn mutation operators using human analysis of equivalence (ICSE 2014)
- **What it does:** the human ground-truth study of which operators produce equivalent (un-killable) and stubborn (hard but killable) mutants.
- **Method / data:** **4,181** generated mutants over **18** programs; the **1,230** mutants unkilled by branch-adequate suites were manually classified with an explicit decision procedure; 5-operator family (ABS, UOI, LCR, ROR, …).
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** PARAPHRASE: "Of 4,181 generated mutants, 1,230 unkilled mutants were manually checked; **946 (≈23%)** were equivalent and **284 (≈7%)** stubborn." Operator distribution is highly uneven: the **ABS class and half the UOI class generate many equivalent and almost no stubborn mutants**, while **LCR generates many stubborn and few equivalent mutants**. Few equivalent mutants are caused by dead code.
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no coverage-conditioned scoring protocol; nothing at system level.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none. It is the source of our operator blacklist (ABS, post-inc/dec UOI).

### Chekam2017FaultRevelation — An Empirical Study on Mutation, Statement and Branch Coverage Fault Revelation that Avoids the Unreliable Clean Program Assumption (ICSE 2017)
- **What it does:** compares fault revelation of statement coverage, branch coverage, weak and strong mutation while removing the "clean program assumption" (recomputing coverage per faulty version).
- **Method / data:** 5 Unix utilities with real faults; 10,000 sampling repetitions per fault/criterion; custom KLEE-based mutation tooling.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - "our primary finding is that strong mutation testing has the highest fault revelation of four widely-used criteria."
  - "**21** faults are uniquely coupled to strong mutation … (showing that strong mutation uniquely couples to **38%** of faults that are coupled to any of the four criteria). By contrast, each of the other three criteria has no faults uniquely coupled, and even considering all three together only have two faults that are not coupled to strong mutation."
  - "fault revelation starts to increase significantly only once relatively high levels of coverage are attained."
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** C utilities; no system-level suites; no score-comparability angle.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; it is the core empirical warrant for "coverage first, then mutation as the oracle signal".

### Kintis2018DetectingTrivial — Detecting Trivial Mutant Equivalences via Compiler Optimisations (IEEE TSE 2018)
- **What it does:** TCE — identify equivalent and duplicate mutants by comparing compiler-optimised forms.
- **Method / data:** C and Java programs plus a ground-truth benchmark of known equivalent mutants.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "TCE equivalent mutants account for **7.4** and **5.7** percent of all C and Java mutants, while duplicated mutants account for a further **21** percent of all C mutants and **5.4** percent Java mutants"; against the ground truth, "approximately **30** percent (for C) and **54** percent (for Java) are TCE equivalent"; "results differ between languages, since mutation characteristics are language-dependent".
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** cannot be applied cross-language without a per-language check; no Python/JS numbers.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; gives us a cheap pre-filter and a lower bound on residual equivalence.

### Kushigian2024EquivalentWild — Equivalent Mutants in the Wild (ISSTA 2024)
- **What it does:** fresh ground truth on equivalent-mutant types and prevalence in real Java projects, plus a suppressor (EMS) beating TCE.
- **Method / data:** **1,992** sampled mutants from **7** OSS Java projects → **215** equivalents; EMS (10 analyses) evaluated on **19** projects; "all **9,047** equivalent mutants reported by any tool" analysed; EMS detects **8,776** equivalents within **325 s**.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "(1) the median equivalent mutant rate across the 7 projects is **2.97%**; (2) many equivalent mutants are caused by common programming patterns and their detection is not much more complex than structural pattern matching over an abstract syntax tree."
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** Java only; sampling-based ground truth; nothing about score comparability or suite level.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; gives us the modern prevalence number to cite (≈3%, far below the 23% of Yao 2014 — the discrepancy is operator- and corpus-dependent and must be discussed).

### Sanchez2022MutationInTheWild — Mutation testing in the wild: findings from GitHub (EMSE 2022)
- **What it does:** the empirical picture of which mutation tools practitioners actually adopt.
- **Method / data:** "we first performed a search for mutation testing tools, **127** in total"; automatic GitHub mining; manual classification of "over **3.5K** GitHub active repositories" for the top ten tools.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "we observed a recent upturn in interest and activity, with **Infection (PHP), PIT (Java) and Humbug (PHP)** being the most widely used mutation tools in recent years"; predominant uses are development, then teaching/learning, then research; tool-popularity differs sharply between literature and GitHub.
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no scores, no comparability analysis, no JS/Python focus.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; grounds our claim that PIT is the de-facto Java standard.

### Leotta2023Mutta — Mutta: a novel tool for E2E web mutation testing (Software Quality Journal 2023)
- **What it does:** automates mutation testing for whole web applications driven by end-to-end suites, and uses it to compare assertion-based vs differential E2E testing.
- **Method / data:** 2 web applications, "more than **15k** overall" mutants, "about **87k** tests runs overall"; mutated server source deployed and full E2E suite re-run.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "Mutta … generated a large number of mutants (more than 15k overall), took into account the coverage information to consider only the mutants actually executed, deployed the mutated web app, ran the entire E2E test suites (about 87k tests runs overall)". Per-application mutation scores are reported in the paper (not extracted here).
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no public-corpus study, no BDD/Gherkin distinction, no BDD-only vs full-suite separation, no longitudinal drift.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** **minor** — it establishes that system-level mutation scoring is feasible and that others also condition on coverage, so we must position our contribution as corpus + domain-layer restriction + BDD-only/full-suite decomposition + erosion over time.

### Guerino2024PythonTools — Static and Dynamic Comparison of Mutation Testing Tools for Python (SBQS 2024)
- **What it does:** cross-compares Python mutation tools statically (features) and dynamically (cross-testing each tool's adequate suites against the others' mutants).
- **Method / data:** "four Python mutation testing tools, namely **CosmicRay, MutPy, MutMut, and Mutatest**"; test suites built from Pynguin, improved with LLMs and manual analysis, then cross-tested across tools.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "CosmicRay offers superior functionalities and customization options for mutant generation compared to its counterparts. Although CosmicRay's performance was slightly lower than MutPy in the dynamic [evaluation]…" (numeric tables not extracted).
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no absolute-score comparability claim, no BDD, no JS.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; supplies the Python-side comparability caveat (mutmut is one of four non-equivalent tools).

### Kintis2018HowEffectiveTools — How effective are mutation testing tools? (EMSE 2018)
- **What it does:** the most direct published cross-tool comparison of Java mutation tools, using both real faults and manual mutant analysis.
- **Method / data:** **6,493** manually analysed mutants; four tools (PIT, PIT-RV, MuJava, Major); Defects4J real faults; manual equivalence classification per tool.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):**
  - "We find that there are large differences between the tools' effectiveness and demonstrate that **no tool is able to subsume the others**."
  - equivalent mutants produced: **MuJava 203, Major 94, PIT-RV 382, PIT 43**; test cases needed to kill: **MuJava 138, Major 97, PIT-RV 105, PIT 80**.
  - fault revelation at the 100%-coverage control: **PIT-RV 91%, MuJava 85%, Major 80%**; PIT-RV finds "approximately **6%** more real faults" than the others.
  - "existing tools have a much lower effectiveness than what they should or what researchers believe they ought [to have]".
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** Java only; no config normalisation recipe; nothing on JS/Python.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none — and it is the decisive answer to our question (c).

### Just2012RedundantMutants — Do redundant mutants affect the effectiveness and efficiency of mutation analysis? (ICST/Mutation 2012)
- **What it does:** quantifies how redundant mutants inflate mutation score and cost.
- **Method / data:** 4 real-world programs, 3,000–40,000 LOC; redundant-free redefinition of COR.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "the conditional operator replacement (COR) and relational operator replacement (ROR) mutation operators that create **41%** of all mutants … the removal of redundant mutants reduces the runtime of mutation analysis by up to **34%**"; "redundant mutants can lead to a mutation score that is misleadingly overestimated by as much as **10%**".
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no cross-suite comparison; no system level.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; supplies the magnitude of the redundancy bias we must control.

### Ojdanic2023MutationEvolving — Mutation Testing in Evolving Systems: Studying the Relevance of Mutants to Code Evolution (ACM TOSEM 2023)
- **What it does:** defines and measures *commit-relevant* mutants (mutants whose effect interacts with a program change), i.e. the mutation analogue of our "drift over time" question.
- **Method / data:** "over 10 million" mutants, **288** commits, **5** open-source projects, "over **68,213** CPU days of computation"; commit-relevance approximated by observational slicing via higher-order mutation.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "commit-relevant mutants are located mainly outside of program commit change (**81%**)"; effective selection of commit-relevant mutants "has the potential of reducing the number of mutants by up to **93%**"; commit-aware mutation testing beats random mutant selection and "mutants within the program change" baselines; "most proxy features do not reliably predict commit-relevant mutants".
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** unit-level Java/C, no suite-level oracle-strength claim, no BDD, no *erosion of oracle strength* over releases (it studies mutant relevance to commits, not whether suites lose checking power as specs drift).
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** **minor** — it is the paper most likely to be cited against a naive "nobody has studied mutation over time" claim. We must phrase novelty as *oracle-strength erosion of BDD suites*, not as "mutation over evolution", and we must not mutate only changed lines (81% of relevant mutants live outside the change).

### Straubinger2024ManualEquivalent — An Empirical Evaluation of Manually Created Equivalent Mutants (ICSTW 2024)
- **What it does:** asks whether *humans* also create equivalent mutants, using the Code Defenders game.
- **Method / data:** mutants and tests produced by players; manual inspection plus automated identification methods.
- **Key findings (verbatim numbers only):** "less than **10%** of manually created mutants are equivalent"; "a significant portion of developers struggle to accurately identify equivalent mutants".
- **What it does NOT answer for us:** no tool-level prevalence, no protocol for our pipeline.
- **Threat it poses to our novelty:** none; useful only to argue that equivalence is a property of the *mutant generator*, so a tool-produced ≈3% (Kushigian 2024) is a tool artefact we must report rather than assume.

## DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

### (a) Defensible justification for treating domain-layer mutation score as "oracle strength"

Three independent lines of evidence converge, and they must be cited **together** or the construct looks like a convenience metric:

1. **Mutation score is not a coverage proxy.** Just et al. 2014 show the mutant-detection↔real-fault-detection
   correlation holds *independently of code coverage* and is significantly stronger than the
   statement-coverage↔fault-detection correlation (all subjects except Joda-Time). Jain et al. 2023
   supply the mechanism: correlation with coverage is 0.7479 over all files but collapses to **0.2066**
   (R² = 0.021) once you restrict to *covered* lines. **Therefore: conditioning on coverage is precisely
   what converts mutation score from a coverage re-measurement into an oracle measurement.**
2. **Mutation finds what coverage cannot.** Chekam et al. 2017: strong mutation uniquely couples to
   **38%** of the faults coupled to *any* of four criteria, while statement, branch and weak mutation
   together have zero uniquely-coupled faults (and only 2 faults uncoupled from strong mutation).
   Chekam also shows fault revelation only rises at high coverage — i.e. the signal is only worth
   reading *after* coverage is exhausted, which is exactly the regime of a suite that fully executes
   the domain layer through step definitions.
3. **Mutants remain coupled to real faults in modern, large repositories.** Petrović et al. 2021:
   **70%** of 1,502 high-priority Google bugs would have had a live fault-coupled mutant on the
   bug-introducing change; Just et al. 2014: **73%** of 357 real faults are coupled. Andrews et al. 2005
   is the historical warrant ("mutants are similar to real faults").

**Scope restriction we can defend, and its cost.** Restricting to the domain layer is *substantively*
justified because the domain layer is where specification-level behaviour is implemented, and it is the
only layer a Gherkin scenario can be *about*. But it must be stated that this restriction inherits the
known blind spots: **17%** of real faults are not coupled to *any* mutant and **27%** are not coupled to
commonly used operators (Just 2014), with the non-coupled classes being algorithmic change,
code deletion and "bugs in higher-level specifications or protocols" (Petrović 2021: "no such mutation
operator" 23/50). Mutation score is therefore a **lower-bound-flavoured** index of oracle strength, not
an estimate of fault detection — and it is *comparative*, not absolute.

**Also answer the "score is not actionable" critique head-on.** Petrović et al. 2022 (Google) explicitly
abandoned the project-level mutation score ("we were also unable to find a good way to surface it to the
developers in an actionable way"). Our defence is that we do not use it as a developer goal; we use it as
a **relative index across suites and across time**, which is exactly the use Just et al. 2014 validated
("we do not interpret absolute mutation scores").

### (b) What we MUST report to survive the standard criticisms

Papadakis et al. 2019 give a **seven-point checklist**; we should map our protocol onto it item by item, and
add the equivalent-mutant and subsumption items that the checklist handles only indirectly:

| # | Requirement | Our obligation | Evidence for why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Operator choice justified** | Report the exact operator set per tool; exclude ABS and post-inc/dec UOI (disproportionate equivalents); always include COR/ROR/SBR | Yao 2014 (ABS & half UOI: many equivalents, almost no stubborn; LCR: many stubborn, few equivalent); Just 2014 (COR/ROR/SBR most often coupled to real faults); Petrović 2022 (AOR/LCR/ROR/UOI/SBR) |
| 2 | **Tool justified + exact version** | Name tool and version; state that scores are not comparable across tools | Papadakis 2019 ("tools differ significantly"); Kintis 2018 EMSE (equiv counts 43→382 across four Java tools; fault revelation 80–91%; no tool subsumes another) |
| 3 | **Redundancy controlled** | Use non-redundant operator definitions and/or TCE-style duplicate removal; report the residual | Just 2012 (redundancy inflates score by up to 10%; COR+ROR = 41% of mutants); Papadakis 2016 (62% Type I error; 68% of 17 papers vulnerable; <5% of mutants subsuming) |
| 4 | **Test-suite choice and size** | Report suite size, and hold suite size constant or normalise when correlating | Papadakis 2019 item 4 |
| 5 | **Clean-program assumption** | State that mutants are run against the clean program (we do) and what that implies | Papadakis 2019 item 5; Chekam 2017 shows CPA can flip conclusions |
| 6 | **Repetitions** | State determinism: our pipeline has no stochastic step *except mutant sampling*; if sampling, report the number of repetitions/samples | Papadakis 2019 item 6 |
| 7 | **Granularity of results** | Report at the granularity we claim (suite-level *and* domain-layer), not one score per project | Papadakis 2019 item 7; Jain 2023 (file-level distribution differs from project-level) |
| + | **Equivalent-mutant handling** | State explicitly: detection is undecidable; report the suppression method (TCE/EMS or manual audit of a random sample, with sample size and agreement), and report an estimated equivalence rate | Papadakis 2019 §5.3.1; Kintis 2018 TCE (**5.7%** Java); Kushigian 2024 (median **2.97%**); Yao 2014 (**≈23%** of generated under a 5-operator set with ABS/UOI) |
| + | **Sampling protocol** | If mutants are sampled under a budget: report sampling rate, strata, random seed, and (ideally) a stability check | Jain 2023 (12.9% sample rate; up to 100 mutants/file); Petrović 2021/2022 (1 mutant/line; >90% of lines have 100% majority fate, so 1/line is defensible); Just 2012 (10% inflation risk) |
| + | **Coverage conditioning** | Report coverage alongside every mutation score; report *covered-only* mutation score | Jain 2023 (0.7479 vs 0.2066); Google only mutates covered code; Mutta only considers executed mutants; Chekam 2017 |

Two further disclosures that the literature effectively demands and that most papers omit:

- **Unproductive-mutant rate.** Report the fraction of surviving mutants that are unproductive/trivial
  (Google's pre-filtering figure was **85%**; post-filter **11%**). Reviewers who know Petrović 2022 will
  ask; answering pre-empts the "your score is mostly noise" objection.
- **BDD-only vs full-suite kill must be reported as a decomposition, not a single number**, because the
  interesting quantity is the *incremental* kill attributable to the BDD layer over whatever unit tests
  the repository also ships.
- **State what we mutate over time.** Ojdanic et al. 2023 show that **81%** of commit-relevant mutants lie
  *outside* the changed lines, so a purely diff-based (incremental) mutation strategy — the industry
  standard after Google — is the wrong instrument for measuring *erosion* of oracle strength. Erosion must
  be measured on a **fixed, stable mutant set per domain module at each release**, not on changed lines,
  otherwise a drop in score is indistinguishable from a change in what we mutated. This also gives us a
  principled answer to "why not just use CI-based incremental mutation testing".

### (c) Are scores from different mutation tools comparable? — **NO** (published evidence says the opposite)

- **Kintis et al. 2018 (EMSE)** is the decisive study: four Java tools on the same subjects and real
  faults produce **203 / 94 / 382 / 43** equivalent mutants respectively, need **138 / 97 / 105 / 80**
  test cases to be killed, and reach **91% / 85% / 80%** fault revelation at a 100%-coverage control;
  "no tool is able to subsume the others". A mutation score computed by PIT is *not* the same
  measurement as one computed by MuJava or Major.
- **Papadakis et al. 2019**: "mutation testing tools differ significantly"; the checklist demands the
  tool be justified and its version reported.
- **Guerino et al. 2024 (SBQS)**: four Python tools (CosmicRay, MutPy, MutMut, Mutatest) are
  feature- and behaviour-divergent; suites adequate for one tool do not transfer cleanly.
- **Kushigian et al. 2024**: even *equivalent-mutant sets* differ by the tool that reported them
  (9,047 tool-reported equivalents; EMS finds 8,776) — the denominators themselves are tool artefacts.
- **Unknown:** there is **no** published calibration study mapping PIT↔Stryker↔mutmut scores, and no
  normalisation recipe. **Practical consequence for us:** compare scores only *within* one tool and one
  configuration; if our corpus spans languages (Java/JS/Python), report **per-language, per-tool** scores
  and never pool raw percentages across tools; or restrict to one language and say so.

### (d) Prior work measuring mutation score of *acceptance-level / system-level* suites

- **Yes, but thin and tool-oriented.** **Mutta** (Leotta et al., SQJ 2023) is the only paper I opened that
  measures mutation score for **end-to-end** suites: 2 web apps, **>15k** mutants, **~87k** E2E test runs,
  and it *also* restricts to coverage-reached mutants. Its purpose is comparing assertion-based vs
  differential E2E testing — not measuring a corpus of public suites, not BDD, not longitudinal.
- **Jain et al. 2023 explicitly exclude system/integration tests** ("We exclude integration or system
  tests in these experiments … System and integration tests are not as simply structured as unit tests,
  making it difficult-to-impossible to programatically systematically vary test coverage/oracle strength").
  This is the clearest published statement that system-level oracle measurement is an open gap — and it
  is a *quotable* justification for our contribution.
- Every other measurement study I opened (Just 2014; Chekam 2017; Yao 2014; Kintis 2018 EMSE;
  Just 2012; Jain 2023) operates on **unit/class-level** suites of Java or C programs. The industrial
  studies (Petrović 2021/2022) also mutate at changelist/unit-test granularity.
- **Verdict:** measuring mutation score of acceptance-level suites has been *done once, by a tool paper,
  on two applications*; measuring it on a **corpus of public Cucumber-style suites, restricted to the
  domain layer, decomposed into BDD-only vs full-suite kill, and tracked over time** has **not** been
  done. Our novelty is intact, with Mutta as the mandatory nearest-neighbour citation and Jain 2023's
  exclusion statement as the gap warrant.
