Do Public BDD Suites Still Work as Oracles? A
Mutation-Based Instrument for Detecting
Executable-Specification Drift in Cucumber-Style Suites
Anonymous submission — author identities withheld for review
Abstract. [Context] A business rule lives in three places that can drift apart: the organisational requirement,
the executable specification written in Gherkin, and the code. Behaviour-Driven Development (BDD) is
adopted on the premise that executable specifications keep the first and third aligned, yet no metric is
used to check whether that alignment still holds. [Objective] We ask whether the specification layer of a
Cucumber-style suite still functions as an oracle — whether it can discriminate a change in the business rules
it claims to describe — and we design an instrument for organisations to answer it about their own suites.
[Method] Following Design Science Research, we build and evaluate a drift-detection protocol as feasibility
of mutation-based oracle measurement in two ecosystems, and a measurement of the public population
from a frame of 2,552 repositories drawn with a fixed seed under a categorised attrition protocol. [Results]
Mutation-based oracle measurement is feasible in the Java and JavaScript/TypeScript ecosystems, with
per-mutant coverage and per-scenario attribution. However, of 2,552 screened repositories 94 met the study’s
static eligibility criteria (3.68%; 95% CI 3.0–4.5%), and of 41 build-tested repositories 0 produced a green,
executing Gherkin baseline. [Conclusion/Contributions] The binding constraint on evidence about BDD
oracle quality is infrastructural rather than statistical: public BDD suites are overwhelmingly frameworks,
templates and demonstrators, not applications with an executable specification layer. We contribute the
instrument, the feasibility evidence and a quantified attrition analysis. Limitation. No independent human
raters were available, so the judgement-coded indicators carry no inter-rater reliability statistic, and no
corpus-scale association could be estimated.
Keywords: Behaviour-Driven Development, executable specifications, mutation testing, test oracle, specifi-
cation drift, empirical software engineering

1 Introduction
An information system is not merely a body of code;
it is a system of work in which organisational rules are
encoded so that routine decisions can be made consis-
tently. When a lender approves a loan, a retailer prices
an order, or an insurer settles a claim, the software en-
acts a business rule that someone, somewhere, wrote
down. The distance between the rule as intended and
the rule as enacted is an old concern of the Information
Systems (IS) field, whose literature has long treated the
alignment between business strategy and information
technology as a continuing managerial problem rather
than a one-off design decision Henderson and Venkatra-
man (1993), and which distinguishes the quality of the
information an artefact carries from the quality of the
system that carries it DeLone and McLean (2003). This
paper is about a specific, measurable, and surprisingly
neglected way in which that distance grows.
Behaviour-Driven Development (BDD) was pro-
posed precisely to close it. A requirement is written
as a concrete scenario in a structured natural language
(Gherkin); each step of the scenario is bound to code;
and the scenario is executed as a test. The promise is
that the specification is the test, so the specification can-
not silently diverge from the implementation. BDD is
now widely used in industry, and empirical work has con-
firmed its adoption in open-source projects: in a study
of 50,000 popular repositories, approximately 27% used
a BDD framework, rising to 68% among Ruby projects
Zampetti et al. (2020).
The promise has a structural weakness. A Gherkin
scenario is only an oracle if its steps actually check
something that could fail. Scenarios can be tagged
@wip and skipped; assertions inside step definitions can
be removed, loosened, or written so that the observed
value is compared against a value computed by the
very production code under test. When that happens
the scenario still runs, still passes, still contributes to
coverage — and no longer detects the change it exists to
detect. The test has become a mirror of the defect rather
than a check on it. Coverage metrics cannot see this,
because coverage measures reach, not discrimination.
The consequence for practice is uncomfortable: an
organisation may believe it holds an executable spec-
ification of its business rules while in fact holding a
set of scripts that would pass under almost any imple-
mentation. The consequence for IS research is an open
measurement question. Is there an instrument that tells
an organisation, with acceptable effort, whether its spec-
ification layer still functions as an oracle — and whether
that ability is decaying?

1.1 The gap this paper addresses
The literature tells us a great deal about BDD’s adoption
Zampetti et al. (2020), its scenario quality Chandorkar
et al. (2022); Binamungu et al. (2020), the maintain-
ability burden of keeping scenarios and code in step
Binamungu et al. (2018b); Oliveira et al. (2019), and
its benefits as reported by practitioners Couto et al.
(2022). It tells us that mutation analysis is the standard
instrument for measuring a suite’s discriminating power,
and that mutation scores track real fault detection in a
way coverage does not Just et al. (2014). It also tells us
that tests decay: disabled tests are common and often
never re-enabled Kim et al. (2021b), test cases have a
measurable half-life Feldt (2014), and tests can pass
while checking nothing Delplanque et al. (2019); Zhi and
Garousi (2013).
What the literature does not provide is the intersec-
tion. A recent systematic mapping of 166 BDD papers
published between 2006 and 2021 found no treatment
of mutation analysis, and explicitly named an “acute
shortage of metrics for measuring various aspects of
BDD specifications” Binamungu and Maro (2023). The
closest existing work applied mutation analysis to a
BDD acceptance suite exactly once, in a single indus-
trial robotic-system proof of concept with hand-selected
domain mutants dos Santos et al. (2024). To our knowl-
edge, no study has measured, across a corpus of public
Cucumber-style suites, how much discriminating power
the specification layer contributes relative to the rest of
the test suite, nor how that contribution changes over
repository history. This paper is an attempt to build
the instrument for that measurement, and an honest re-
port of what happened when we pointed it at the public
ecosystem.
1.2 Research questions
The study is organised around three questions that sep-
arate whether we can measure from what we find:
RQ1 (Measurement validity). Can specification drift be
operationalised as a reproducible metric set, and
are the resulting measurements reliable and tool-
feasible across ecosystems?
RQ2 (Oracle strength). What share of covered, domain-
layer mutants does the BDD suite alone kill, and
how much does it add beyond the repository’s other
tests?
RQ3 (Association and erosion). Do pre-specified static
drift indicators associate with lower BDD-only kill
rates, and does drift accumulate over repository
history?
A word on vocabulary, because it matters for hon-
esty. We use oracle strength for the share of covered
domain-layer mutants killed by the specification layer.
This is a snapshot property. Drift is a change over time:
a suite whose oracle strength is low at one commit may
simply always have been weak. We are careful through-
out not to infer drift from a single measurement, and
the term specification drift is used here as a definition
we introduce, not as an established construct in the
literature (Section 3 explains the established relatives:
architectural drift and erosion Perry and Wolf (1992);
Rosik et al. (2010), code decay Izurieta and Bieman
(2012)).
1.3 Contributions
1. An instrument. A five-step protocol
(docs/PROTOCOL.md) that a team can apply
to its own suite: delimit the domain layer, measure
oracle strength in BDD-only and full-suite modes
with mandatory coverage conditioning, compute
seven drift indicators, test for accumulation over
history, and interpret under explicit rules that
forbid the most common misreadings (Section 4,
Section 5).
2. Tool-feasibility evidence, established empirically
rather than assumed. Mutation analysis driven
by the BDD runner itself, with per-mutant cov-
erage and per-scenario attribution, is demon-
strated in both the Java ecosystem (PIT with the
Cucumber-JVM JUnit-Platform engine) and the
JavaScript/TypeScript ecosystem (Stryker with the
official Cucumber runner). We report the exact
pinned versions and the observed mutation counts.
3. A pre-specified, quantified account of the popula-
tion. From a frame of 2,552 repositories enumerated
without a popularity-ranked search, we report eligi-
bility, build and execution attrition with categorised
reasons, and show that the binding constraint on
this literature is infrastructural rather than statisti-
cal.
4. A negative result and its implications. The claim
that public BDD suites can serve as the empirical
basis for a corpus-scale study of oracle quality does
not survive contact with the ecosystem. We quan-
tify why, and we state what a future study would
have to do instead.
1.4 What this paper does not claim
We do not claim that BDD is ineffective, nor that public
BDD suites are of poor quality. We do not establish
that BDD causes better or worse oracles: the design is
observational and observational designs cannot support
that claim. We do not measure defects or field failures;
mutation score is a proxy for the ability to detect a
change in business-rule behaviour, not a count of real
bugs. And we do not report inter-rater reliability for the
two judgement-coded indicators, because no independent
human rater was available; we say so explicitly rather
than reporting a number we could not honestly produce
(Section 8).

2 Background: why this is an
Information Systems problem
2.1 The rule, the specification and the
code
In the IS tradition an information system is understood
as a system of work in which human participants and
technological components jointly perform activities to
process information and support decision-making, and
its value emerges from integration into organisational
practice rather than from technological functionality
alone. The alignment between business strategy and
information technology has been framed as a continuing
process of managerial fit rather than a one-off design
choice Henderson and Venkatraman (1993), and the
success of an information system has been modelled as
a multi-dimensional construct in which the quality of
the information and the quality of the system are dis-
tinct from their use and their net organisational benefit
DeLone and McLean (2003). These framings matter
here for a direct reason: they insist that a system can
be technically sound and organisationally wrong at the
same time, which is precisely the failure mode this paper
measures.
BDD is an attempt to make that correspondence
inspectable. A requirement is expressed as scenarios in
Gherkin, in domain vocabulary, and those scenarios are
bound to executable steps. Three artefacts are then in
play, and each can move independently:
• the organisational rule (what the business intends,
possibly recorded only in a policy document or a
verbal agreement);
• the executable specification (the Gherkin scenarios,
which are supposed to state the rule in checkable
form);
• the implementation (the code that actually decides
outcomes).
BDD’s premise is that the second artefact binds
the first and the third: change the code without chang-
ing the scenarios and the suite fails. The premise fails
quietly when the second artefact loses its discriminating
power. A scenario whose step definition asserts nothing,
or asserts that a value equals itself, or compares the
actual output with a value recomputed using the same
production code, will not fail when the implementation
changes — because the oracle moved with the implemen-
tation. The specification is then descriptively present
and normatively absent.
2.2 Why coverage cannot detect this
The IS literature on system evaluation is familiar with
the difference between utilisation and effectiveness. Test
coverage has the same character: it records that code
was exercised, not that exercising it could have pro-
duced a failure. The empirical record is unambiguous.
In an industrial Ruby project, mutation scores aver-
aged 0.78 while statement coverage averaged 97.2% Li
et al. (2015). Across 45 Maven projects, the correlation
between coverage and mutation score was 0.75 when
computed naïvely but fell to 0.21 once restricted to cov-
ered code Jain et al. (2023) — that is, almost all of
the apparent association was an artefact of which code
was covered, and among code that is covered, coverage
says very little about discrimination. Mutation score,
by contrast, tracks real fault detection independently of
coverage Just et al. (2014), although it remains a proxy:
a substantial minority of real faults have no mutant
representative Just et al. (2014); Chekam et al. (2017).
For an organisation the practical question is there-
forenot“whatisourcoverage?” but“ifsomeonechanged
this business rule, would anything fail?” That is the
question mutation analysis answers, and it is the ques-
tion this paper operationalises for the specification layer
specifically.
2.3 Decay is the normal case, not the
exception
That test artefacts degrade is well documented. Dis-
abled tests are common — 3,111 disabling changes across
122,000 commits in 15 Java systems, with 41% of dis-
abled tests never re-enabled and a median 39 days to
re-enable Kim et al. (2021b). Test cases have a half-life
of five to twelve months in a large industrial setting
Feldt (2014). A substantial share of “green” tests check
less than their authors believe: 294 rotten green tests
were found in 19,905 test cases Delplanque et al. (2019),
and inadequate assertions have been catalogued as a
recurring defect pattern Zhi and Garousi (2013); Zhang
and Mesbah (2015). Test smells are pervasive — 86% of
JUnit tests in one study exhibited at least one Bavota
et al. (2015) — and their removal is mostly incidental
rather than deliberate Kim et al. (2021a).
If decay is the normal case for tests in general,
there is no reason to expect the specification layer of
a BDD suite to be exempt. It is, however, a layer
with a distinctive failure mode: because the scenario
text remains readable and business-facing even when
the assertions behind it have been hollowed out, the
artefact continues to look like a specification long after
it has stopped functioning as one. That asymmetry —
legible appearance, degraded function — is what makes
an instrument necessary, and it is why a practitioner
cannot simply read the feature files and know.
2.4 The Design Science framing
We therefore treat the problem as a design problem,
in the sense of Design Science Research (DSR). Design
science builds and evaluates artefacts intended to ad-
dress identified organisational problems, with relevance
and rigour as twin requirements Hevner et al. (2004),
and it is distinguished from natural-science research by
the kind of knowledge it produces: the one builds arte-
facts, the other seeks explanations, and the two have
different truth criteria March and Smith (1995). DSR
is accordingly expected to state the problem class, the

artefact’s design rationale, its evaluation in a relevant
context, and the implications for practice Peffers et al.
(2007); Gregor and Hevner (2013). The response here
is to build and evaluate an artefact that makes the
divergence detectable.
Framing the contribution this way is not a rhetorical
choice. It determines what counts as success. A DSR
artefact is evaluated on whether it solves the problem
class in a relevant context, and a negative finding about
the context — that the population on which the artefact
would be validated at scale barely exists — is a finding
about the artefact’s deployment conditions, not a failure
of the artefact. Section 7 draws out that distinction.
3 Related work and the novelty
claim
We organise prior work into four bodies and state, for
each, what it establishes and what it leaves open. The
section closes with the precise claim this paper defends
and an explicit list of claims it does not make.
3.1 BDD adoption and specification
quality
Empirical work on BDD has concentrated on adoption,
scenariostructureandmaintainability. Thelargeststudy
screened 50,000 popular open-source projects across
five languages, estimated that about 27% use a BDD
framework (68% among Ruby projects), examined co-
evolution between scenarios and production code in 20
Ruby projects using Granger causality — finding co-
evolution in roughly 37% of cases — and surveyed 31
developers, most of whom reported writing tests while
or after coding rather than strictly applying BDD Zam-
pettiet al.(2020). Scenario-levelanalyseshaveexamined
Gherkin structure and smells Chandorkar et al. (2022),
duplicate steps and their refactoring Binamungu et al.
(2018a); Irshad et al. (2022), the effort of keeping step
definitions consistent with features Binamungu et al.
(2018b), and the characterisation of what practition-
ers consider good scenarios Binamungu et al. (2020);
Oliveira and Marczak (2017); Oliveira et al. (2019);
Couto et al. (2022); Farooq et al. (2023). Co-evolution
between tests and production code has also been studied
outside BDD Zaidman et al. (2008); Marsavina et al.
(2014); Sun et al. (2023); Miranda et al. (2025).
The limitation, for our purposes, is uniform across
this body: quality is assessed by structural properties,
by developer opinion, or by co-change statistics — never
by asking whether the suite would fail if the behaviour
changed. A 2023 systematic mapping of 166 BDD papers
from2006to2021makesthisconcrete: thestring“mutat”
does not occur in the review, and the authors identify an
“acute shortage of metrics for measuring various aspects
of BDD specifications” Binamungu and Maro (2023).
3.2 Mutation analysis as a measure of
discriminating power
Mutation analysis is the accepted instrument for mea-
suring whether a suite can detect change. The central
validity question — are mutants a substitute for real
faults? — has been examined directly: across 357 real
faults and roughly 230,000 mutants, mutant detection
was positively correlated with real fault detection inde-
pendently of code coverage, while 17% of faults had no
coupling to any commonly used mutant Justet al. (2014);
earlier work questioned whether mutation is an appro-
priate experimental tool at all Andrews et al. (2005).
Fault revelation has been studied per criterion Chekam
et al. (2017), redundant and equivalent mutants are a
known measurement hazard Just et al. (2012); Yao et al.
(2014); Kintis et al. (2018a); Kushigian et al. (2024);
Straubinger et al. (2024), and mutation testing has been
examined as a practitioner activity Petrovic et al. (2021,
2022). Surveys and threat analyses set out the standards
a credible mutation-based study must meet Papadakis
et al. (2019, 2016); Jia and Harman (2011).
Two results from this body shape our design directly.
First, the coverage–mutation gap: raw correlations be-
tween coverage and mutation score largely disappear
once analysis is conditioned on covered code Jain et al.
(2023), which is why coverage conditioning is mandatory
in our protocol rather than optional. Second, tools are
not interchangeable: the same subjects analysed with
four Java tools produced equivalent-mutant counts of
203, 94, 382 and 43, and no tool subsumed the others
Kintis et al. (2018b). Cross-tool pooling of mutation
scores is therefore indefensible, which is why our analysis
is stratified by language and tool.
Mutation analysis has been applied at levels above
the unit test — to end-to-end web suites Leotta et al.
(2023), to evolving systems over time Ojdanic et al.
(2023b,a); Akour and Alenezi (2020) — and to specifi-
cations themselves Black et al. (2000). It has also been
studied where practitioners actually are: a large-scale
examination of repositories on GitHub characterises how
mutation testing is adopted in the wild Sanchez et al.
(2022), a static and dynamic comparison of Python mu-
tation tools shows that they diverge in the mutants
they generate Guerino et al. (2024), and a study asking
“will my tests tell me if I break this code?” examines
the discriminating power of suites at different test lev-
els Niedermayr et al. (2016). The technique has been
combined with test-driven development in a controlled
experiment Roman and Mnich (2020) and extended
to cyber-physical systems through data-driven analysis
Vigano et al. (2022). What it has not been used for,
systematically, is measuring the discriminating power of
a BDD specification layer across a corpus.
3.3 The oracle problem and assertion
quality
Whether a test can detect a fault depends on its oracle
Barr et al. (2015), and the choice of oracle has been
evaluated empirically, including the use of assertions
specifically Shrestha and Rutherford (2011). Assertion
quality has been studied as a defect source Zhi and
Garousi (2013); Zhang and Mesbah (2015), as a mea-
surable density Catolino et al. (2019), and as a driver
of false confidence in “pseudo-tested” methods that are
covered but not effectively asserted Vera-Perez et al.

suites would catch injected faults Yandrapally and Mes-
bah (2021); Soto-Sanchez et al. (2021). Static indicators
have been used to predict mutation scores at the level of
an individual test case Grano et al. (2021), and removing

3.4 Decay, drift and erosion
The vocabulary of decay is established for architecture
and design, not for test suites. Architectural drift and
erosion were characterised early Perry and Wolf (1992),
with subsequent work on architectural drift detection
Rosik et al. (2010) and architectural evolution Wer-
melinger et al. (2011); code decay and design-pattern
decay have likewise been formalised Izurieta and Bie-
man (2012). On the test side, the relevant evidence
concerns disabled tests Kim et al. (2021b), test-case sur-
vival Feldt (2014), rotten green tests Delplanque et al.
(2019); Aranega et al. (2021), test-smell prevalence and
survival Bavota et al. (2015, 2012); Kim (2020); Kim
et al. (2021a); Spadini et al. (2018, 2020); Pontillo et al.
(2024); Panichella et al. (2022); Cai (2026); Martins et al.
(2023); Fushihara et al. (2023); Moonen et al. (2008);
Athanasiou et al. (2014); Pinto et al. (2012), and co-
change between feature files and code Yang et al. (2019).
Two terminological points must be made plainly,
because the literature does not license the terms we
would otherwise like to use. Specification drift is not an
established term in software engineering: our searches of
Crossref and OpenAlex found no peer-reviewed source
that establishes it. The same holds for test decay, test
erosion and test rot as applied to test suites. We there-
fore introduce specification drift explicitly as a definition
— a specialisation of architectural drift to the executable-
specification layer — and we do not present it as a
received construct. This is a terminological contribution
at best, and we do not overstate it.
3.5 Traceability in the Information
Systems literature
Becausetheinstrumentincludesatraceabilitymeasure—
the fraction of identifiable business rules that at least one
scenario references — it is grounded in the IS literature
on requirements traceability rather than invented for the
occasion. Traceability between requirements and down-
stream artefacts is a long-standing IS concern, motivated
by the need to demonstrate that a system satisfies the
requirements it was built against; a systematic review of
pre-requirements-specification traceability characterises
its motivations and the persistent difficulty of establish-
ing it in practice Mucha et al. (2024). That difficulty is
not confined to plan-driven settings: Brazilian studies
of agile practice report that requirements traceability
remains problematic when requirements are expressed
as user stories and acceptance criteria rather than as
numbered specifications Da Trindade and Lucena (2016),
and that independently evolving systems in a software
ecosystem complicate tracing further Goncalves et al.
(2022).
This body of work is why we treat traceability cov-
erage as an indicator of organisational legibility rather
than as a completeness metric. A scenario that refer-
ences no identifiable rule may still be a good test; what
it cannot do is connect the system’s behaviour to a
stated obligation. The IS framing also explains why the
business-and-IT alignment tradition treats such connec-
tions as processes to be maintained rather than states
to be achieved Henderson and Venkatraman (1993): a
traceability link that is established once and never re-
visited decays exactly as the rest of the specification
does.
3.6 What is new, precisely
The claim this paper defends is a conjunction of four
elements, each individually unclaimed:
No study has measured, on a corpus of pub-
lic Cucumber-style suites, the mutation-based
oracle strength of the BDD layer alone — sepa-
rated from the contribution of the repository’s
other tests — nor how that strength changes
across the repository’s history.
The closest prior work is a single industrial proof of
concept that applied mutation analysis to a BDD accep-
tance suite for a robotic system and reported that 79%
of generated mutants were detected, but used hand-
selected domain mutants, a single system, no public
corpus, no separation of the BDD layer’s contribution
from other tests, and no temporal analysis dos Santos
et al. (2024). An industrial experience report on mu-
tation testing in a Ruby project is sometimes cited as
BDD evidence, but its published artefacts concern Ruby
mutation operators and practitioner adoption; the only
openly available version does not mention Cucumber
or Gherkin at all Li et al. (2015). We therefore do not
describe it as a BDD study. Model-based generation of
Cucumber scenarios Li et al. (2016) addresses a different
question.
Accordingly, we explicitly do not claim that: BDD
quality is unstudied; BDD suites have never been anal-
ysed at scale; mutation testing has never been applied
to a BDD suite; longitudinal mutation analysis is new;
or that nobody has related static test-quality indicators
to mutation scores. Each of these would be false, and
the corresponding prior work is cited above.

4 Research design: a Design
Science framing
4.1 Problem class and relevance
The problem class is organisational rather than com-
putational. Organisations are required, with increas-
ing regularity, to demonstrate that a system behaves
as a specified rule intends — for audit, for regulatory
compliance, for contractual acceptance, or simply for
operational consistency. BDD is one of the instruments
adopted to that end. But an organisation that adopts
BDD acquires a representation of its rules, not a guar-
antee about them, and the representation can retain its
appearance while losing its function.
The relevance of the problem class is a matter of
record in the decay literature: disabled tests, rotten
green tests and inadequate assertions are normal, not
pathological Kim et al. (2021b); Delplanque et al. (2019);
Zhi and Garousi (2013). The specificity of the BDD
case is the appearance/function asymmetry described
in Section 2: a .feature file reads like a specification
to a business stakeholder even when its step definitions
stopped checking anything. A manager reviewing the
artefact sees compliance; the instrument we propose
tests whether compliance is real.
4.2 The artefact
Following the design-science convention of describing
an artefact’s rationale and evaluation rather than only
its implementation Hevner et al. (2004); Peffers et al.
(2007), and being explicit about the type of knowledge
the contribution claims Gregor and Hevner (2013), we
state the artefact as a protocol with five steps:
1. Delimit the domain layer using a three-tier rule
(path convention, framework exclusion, decision-
bearing fallback), so that mutation is applied where
business decisions live rather than where data is
moved (Section 5, protocol step 1).
2. Measure oracle strength twice, in BDD-only and
full-suite modes, with coverage conditioning manda-
tory and with the differential attributed to the
specification layer explicitly (protocol step 2).
3. Compute seven drift indicators with fixed polarity
and operational definitions, two of them primary
and five exploratory (protocol step 3).
4. Test for accumulation over history using a determin-
istic sample of commits and a robust trend statistic,
plus a two-snapshot change estimate on a common
mutant set (protocol step 4).
5. Interpret under rules that forbid the common mis-
readings — most importantly, that “not covered”
is not “drifted”, that a snapshot is not a trend,
and that no association below the detectable-effect
floor should be presented as evidence of absence
(protocol step 5).
The full protocol is a deliverable of this work
(docs/PROTOCOL.md) and is written for practitioners,
not for reviewers. Three design decisions in it deserve
explicit defence because they each foreclose a convenient
shortcut:
Coverage conditioning is mandatory, not advisory.
Given that the raw coverage–mutation correlation
largely vanishes once analysis is restricted to
covered code Jain et al. (2023), a study that
reports only an overall mutation score is measuring
coverage under another name. We therefore require
both values side by side in every table.
Differential attribution is required. A suite can be
strong for reasons unrelated to BDD: a mature
project with excellent unit tests will have a high
overall mutation score whether or not its Gherkin
layer checks anything. Reporting only the total
would confound BDD with general engineering qual-
ity. The differential DK norm on a common mutant
set is the artefact’s answer to this, and the paper
states plainly that no causal claim about BDD is
available from an observational design.
Stratification by tool is required. Because mutation
tools are not interchangeable Kintis et al. (2018b),
pooling scores across ecosystems would manufac-
ture a common scale that does not exist. The
artefact therefore forbids a single pooled correlation
as the headline statistic.
4.3 Evaluation plan
The artefact is evaluated on two dimensions, and we are
explicit about which succeeded and which did not:
• Constructed and tested (feasibility). Can the mea-
surement be performed at all with available, pinned
tooling, in more than one ecosystem, with the per-
mutant coverage and per-scenario attribution the
artefact requires? This is answered by construction:
we built and ran the harnesses, and we report the
observed mutation counts and scores (Section 6).
• Applied to a population (attrition and observed
values). What happens when the instrument is
pointed at a defined frame of public repositories un-
der pre-specified eligibility rules? This is answered
by observation, and the answer includes how many
candidate repositories could not be measured and
why. Section 6 reports the funnel; the interpreta-
tion of an instrument whose population is thin is
taken up in Section 7.

5 Method
The method was specified in advance in a versioned pro-
tocol document (docs/method.md), written and commit-
ted before any corpus measurement in order to constrain
analytic freedom. It was not externally registered and
not frozen: no registration service was used, no hash-
frozen copy was lodged, and the document continued to
be edited as the pilot exposed problems. We therefore de-
scribe the work as pre-specified rather than pre-registered,
and we do not claim the evidential force that a genuine
pre-registration would carry.
This matters because the executed protocol departs
from the written one on several load-bearing points,
and those departures are the reader’s business. The
most consequential are: the planned census of a sampled
frame became a full enumeration of the frame; the eligi-
bility criteria were revised after the pilot, replacing the
protocol’s framework-exclusion and domain-layer tests
with an observable application-shape test; the seeded-
drift campaign and the inter-rater reliability study were
not carried out; and the pre-registered stop-at-n rule
was superseded by the available population. Each de-
parture is recorded with a reason and a timestamp in
docs/DEVIATIONS.md and summarised in Section 8; the
replication package contains the protocol document as
written, so a reader can check the differences rather than
take our word for them.
5.1 Sampling frame
A defensible frame must be reproducible, must not be
ranked by popularity, and must not depend on creden-
tials the study does not have. The GitHub code-search
API was unavailable (no access token; the unauthenti-

capped at approximately 1,000 results and ranked by
popularity — characteristics that would have made “the
top results” both unrepresentative and unstable.
We therefore define the frame as follows:
Frame. All public GitHub repositories that
had adopted at least one of twelve declared

cucumber-js, pytest-bdd, specflow,
behavior-driven-development,
bdd-framework, acceptance-testing,

sample of self-identified BDD projects, not of all projects
using Cucumber.

3. at least one step-definition file;

5.4 Outcome: oracle strength
For repository r, let M be the generated domain-layer
mutants, K the killed mutants and Cov those executed
by the run in question. Statuses are taken from the
tool’s per-mutant records, never from its summary line,
because the tools disagree about what their headline
means. All three quantities below are functions of the
run mode; MC denotes mutant coverage.
MS all = |K| +|T| |K| +|S| +|T| +|NC| (1)
MS cov = |K| +|T| |K| +|S| +|T| (2)
MC = |K| +|S| +|T| |K| +|S| +|T| +|NC| (3)
where S denotes surviving, T timed-out and NC
uncovered mutants. Timeouts count as detected in the
primary formula, on the reasoning that a mutant which
prevents termination was distinguished from the orig-
inal; a sensitivity removing them is also reported. In-
valid mutants (compile errors, non-viable mutations)
are excluded from every denominator and their count is
reported. The primary outcome is MS cov(bdd).
Coverage conditioning is mandatory and the follow-
ing rule is binding on every table:
A low MS all produced by low coverage says
that the BDD suite does not execute that code
— “not covered” — and is not evidence of drift,
nor of a weak oracle over the behaviour the
suite does exercise. Only MS cov speaks to the
discriminating power of the suite over the do-
main behaviour it covers.
Differential kill. Let S be the mutants exe-
cuted in both modes. Formally the protocol asserts
executed(bdd) ⊆ executed(full), so S is the BDD-
covered set and every mutant in it is also executed by
the full suite. On that set we report
DK norm = MS cov(full,S)− MS cov(bdd,S) (4)
DK count = |Kfull (S)\Kbdd(S)| |S| (5)
BDDshare = |Kbdd(S)| |Kfull (S)| (6)
DK norm answers what the specification layer adds
beyond the repository’s other tests; BDDshare answers
how much of the full suite’s oracle is the specification
layer. Both are reported, with DK norm as the headline
because it is a difference of scores on an identical mutant
set.
5.5 Drift indicators
Seven indicators are defined so that higher always means
more drift-like. None is sign-flipped after measurement.
Denominators use active scenarios, i.e. all scenarios mi-
nus those already flagged by the skip indicator.

D1 — skip_ratio (primary). A scenario is
skipped if a whole-tag match for @skip, @ignored, @wip,
@pending, @todo, @manual or equivalents appears on the
scenario, its Examples: block, or its feature:
skip_ratio = |{s∈Sr :s is skipped}| |Sr| . (7)
D2 — assertion_weakness_ratio (primary).
The unit is one step definition. A step definition is
STRONG_DIRECT if it carries an assertion token and
matches no weakening pattern; STRONG_DELEGATED if
it carries no token but calls an intra-repository method
that is strong, resolved to depth ≤ 2; WEAK if it carries
a token and matches a weakening pattern; and NONE if
it has neither token nor resolvable callee. Weakening
patterns are tautology (identical operands), constant
truth, unreachable assertion (after an unconditional
return, or in a literal-conditioned branch), swallowed
failure (a catch that neither rethrows nor fails), and
self-comparison. Writing SD, SG, WK and NN for the
four classes:
assertion_weakness = |WK| +|NN| |SD| +|SG| +|WK| +|NN|.
(8)
D3 — stale_spec_ratio. For an active scenario,
let ts be the date of its most recent touching commit
(from git blame over its line range) and ns the number
of commits since ts touching the domain layer. Then
stale = |{s : ages ≥ 180 ∧ns ≥ 5}|/|Ar|, with the
thresholds 180 days and 5 commits fixed before mea-
surement.
D4 — redundancy_cluster_ratio. Active scenar-
ios are fingerprinted by the set of their normalised step
texts (parameters replaced by placeholders), an edge
joins two scenarios at Jaccard ≥ 0.8, and redundancy =
1− (connected components)/|Ar|.
D5 — glue_duplication_ratio. Distinct nor-
malised step invocations divided by total step invoca-
tions across active scenarios. Scripted one-to-one glue
approaches 1; parameterised, reused steps fall well be-
low.
D6 — literal_bearing_stepdef_ratio. The
share of resolved step definitions containing at least
one counted literal: numeric literals other than 0, 1
and −1, and string literals of length ≥ 2. Excluded,
because they are mechanism rather than business con-
stants: booleans and nulls, enum constants, the step
expression itself, assertion messages, log and format
strings, and UI constants (selectors, URLs, XPaths).
D7 — mirror_stepdef_ratio. A mirror step re-
derives the expected value by repeating the production
computation, so that its oracle moves with the mu-
tant. The rater-defined form requires that the asserted
expected value be computed (not a literal), that the com-
putation reference a domain-layer call in the same body,
and that the actual value also come from a domain-layer
call, with the rater citing the domain method inspected.
The automated census computes the loose conjunction
of the first three conditions; its precision is not validated
without human raters and is reported as such.
5.6 Mutation protocol
Mutants are restricted to operators touching decision
logic: conditionals boundary, negated conditionals, arith-
metic, increments, negated values, and return-value
changes. The exact operator set is stated per tool
in the replication package, and no operator set was
altered after seeing results. Mutant sampling, where
required by runtime, uses the fixed study seed under a
per-repository budget. Timeouts, uncovered mutants
and equivalent mutants are counted and reported; none
is silently dropped. The domain layer is delimited by
the three-tier rule of protocol step 1, and the tier used
is recorded per repository.
5.7 Statistics
The statistical plan was fixed before measurement:
• Unit of analysis is the repository. Mutants, sce-
narios and steps are never treated as independent
observations.
• Exactly two primary tests: D1 and D2 against
MS cov(bdd), Holm-corrected across the pair. All
other indicators form one exploratory family, Holm-
corrected within that family and labelled ex-
ploratory in every table.
• No pooled correlation as the headline. Associations
are computed within language/tool strata first, re-
ported as Spearman ρ with bootstrap 95% confi-
dence intervals (2,000 resamples, seed 20261125). A
pooled figure, where given, is the average of within-
stratum associations with a cluster bootstrap by
stratum, and is described as exactly that.
• Coverage is a covariate in every model and appears
in every table.
• Power is reported as a detectable-effect floor. On
the Fisher-z scale with SE(z) = 1.030/√n− 3, the
smallest detectable |ρ| at two-sided α = 0.05 and
power 0.80 is

6.3 Build and execution screen
Static eligibility is necessary but not sufficient: a reposi-
tory must also build and actually execute its Gherkin.
Table 3 reports the outcome of the build and execu-
tion screen over the 41 eligible repositories that were
attempted.
Three observations follow directly from this table
and are important for the interpretation of the whole
study.
First, a green mvn test or npm test does not
prove that a BDD suite ran. The no_bdd_execution
category exists because we initially accepted a zero exit
code as evidence of BDD execution and a spot check
showed this to be false: a repository whose Maven build
succeeded had executed none of its Gherkin. The harness
was changed to require positive evidence of Cucumber
execution in the output before accepting a repository,
and 8 attempted repositories fell into this category. Any
study that equates “tests pass” with “BDD suite passes”
will overcount its corpus.
Second, the failures are heterogeneous, and conflat-
ing them would misstate the evidence. dependency_rot
is a property of the corpus (old pins no longer install);
needs_browser is a property of the harness environ-
ment, not of the suite’s quality; a failing scenario at
HEAD is a property of the repository. Only the last is
a statement about the suite’s health, and only the first
two are avoidable by a better-equipped environment.
Third, the rate of green, executing BDD baselines
in this population is low. We report the count rather
than a rate, because the build screen did not complete
for every eligible repository: of 94 eligible repositories,
41 were attempted. The remaining repositories were not
screened for build and execution within the resources
of this study, and we therefore do not report a green-
suite rate that would imply full coverage of the eligible
set. This is a limitation of our execution budget, not a
finding.

6.7 What could not be measured, and
why it matters
We close the results with an explicit inventory of what
this study did not establish, because a reader is entitled
to know where the evidence stops.
• No inter-rater reliability statistic. The two
judgement-coded indicators (D2’s weakening rubric
and D7’s mirror judgement) require independent
human raters. None were available. No κ is re-
ported, and no automated labeller was substituted,
because agreement between a heuristic and itself is
not inter-rater reliability.
• No validated precision for D7. D7 is reported only
as an automated census of its loose conjunction.
• No completed build screen. 41 of 94 eligible repos-
itories were attempted; the remainder were not
screened for build and execution.
• No corpus-scale association. The pre-specified con-
firmatory family is void under drop rule DR1, be-
cause 0 repositories were measurable against a
threshold of eight per stratum. No association was
estimated, so no null result is claimed either.
• No Python stratum. The frame yielded 5 Python
repositories satisfying the application-shape crite-
rion, and the Python mutation toolchain could not
be established reliably in this environment (package
index unreliability, and the tooling’s coupling to a
unit-test runner rather than a BDD runner). The
planned three-stratum design was therefore not re-
alised, and language effects could not be separated
from tool effects.
7 Discussion
7.1 What the instrument buys an
organisation
The protocol converts an unfalsifiable assurance (“our
scenarios describe the business rules”) into two checkable
quantities: the share of covered domain mutants the
specification layer kills on its own, and the share of the
full suite’s kill that the layer is responsible for. The
second is the one that most organisations have never
computed, and it is the one that answers the question a
manager actually asks — is the Gherkin layer earning
its maintenance cost, or are the unit tests carrying the
suite while the scenarios provide documentation? A high
overall mutation score with a near-zero differential is
a common and previously invisible configuration: the
suite is strong, and the specification layer is decorative.
The coverage-conditioning rule matters here for a
reason that is managerial as much as statistical. A
team told that its mutation score is 45% will reasonably
conclude that its tests are weak. If in fact 40% of
the mutants were never executed by the spec layer —
because the scenarios exercise a thin slice of the domain
— then the correct conclusion is that the scenarios do
not cover the decision logic, which is a coverage problem
with a different and much cheaper remedy than “write
better assertions”. Conflating the two produces the
wrong intervention.
7.2 The finding that dominates: the
population is thin
The most consequential result of this work is not a
correlation. It is that the population on which such a
correlation could be estimated at scale does not readily
exist in public repositories. Of 2,552 screened reposi-
tories, 94 passed a static eligibility test that requires
only features, steps, a build, a licence, an application
shape and a BDD runner. Of those, 0 produced a green,
genuinely executing Gherkin baseline.
Three mechanisms explain this, and they are dis-
tinguishable in the attrition log. First, topic-tagged
BDD repositories are overwhelmingly tooling: reporting
agents, steplibraries, formatters, generators, boilerplates
and demonstration projects. These are legitimate soft-
ware, but they have no business-rule domain to mutate
— a reporting agent has no pricing policy. Second, a large
share of application-shaped repositories are UI-driven:
their scenarios drive a browser, so a green baseline re-
quires a browser, a running application and often seeded
data, none of which a reproducible headless harness can
assume. Third, ordinary dependency rot removes a fur-
ther share: pinned dependency trees from two or three
years ago no longer install.
For the research literature this has a sharp im-
plication. A claim of the form “we studied N public
BDD suites” should be read sceptically unless the au-
thors report their funnel, because the naive population
— repositories carrying a BDD topic tag — is mostly not
composed of the thing being studied. Our contribution
here is to make that funnel visible and categorised, so
that future work can either widen the frame deliberately
or state its own selection more honestly.

7.3 For Design Science: a negative result
about deployment conditions
In DSR terms, the artefact survived its feasibility evalua-
tion and its population evaluation returned a constraint
rather than a correlation. This is not a failure of the
artefact; it is a finding about the context in which the
artefact would be deployed at scale. Two readings are
available and we do not choose between them on the
evidence we have.
The first reading is that the artefact is best deployed
inside organisations, on suites whose build, services and
data the team controls. There, the preconditions of
Section 2 of the protocol can actually be met, and the
differential kill becomes a routine metric rather than a
research instrument. The public-ecosystem study then
serves as a warning: suites that look like specifications
from the outside may not be executable at all, let alone
oracular.
The second reading is that the public BDD ecosys-
tem is genuinely dominated by scaffolding rather than by
specification-bearing applications, and that claims about
“BDD in practice” drawn from public repositories are
therefore systematically skewed towards the scaffolding.
Zampetti et al. already observed that many projects use
BDD frameworks without strictly applying BDD Zam-
petti et al. (2020); our attrition data is consistent with
that observation and adds a mechanical explanation for
why large-scale effectiveness studies are hard to run on
the same population.
7.4 Implications for practice
1. Measure the differential, not the total. A suite-
level mutation score cannot tell you whether the
specification layer is doing any work. Compute
the BDD-only score and the full-suite score on the
same mutant set; the difference is the number worth
reporting to a steering committee.
2. Treat “not covered” and “not checked” as different
defects. They have different remedies and differ-
ent owners. The protocol’s mandatory coverage
conditioning exists to keep them apart.
3. Track skip tags as a first-class metric. The disabled-
test literature shows that disabling is common and
frequently permanent Kim et al. (2021b). A rising
skip ratio is cheap to measure, needs no tooling, and
is the earliest available signal that the specification
layer is being withdrawn from service.
4. Do not assume a scenario checks what its text says.
The appearance/function asymmetry is the whole
problem. A scenario that reads well and asserts
nothing is indistinguishable from a good one in
review; it is distinguishable by mutation.
7.5 Implications for research
The obvious next study is the one this paper could not
run: a corpus of BDD suites drawn from organisations
rather than from public topic tags, where builds, ser-
vices and data are controllable, measured longitudinally
against business-rule change events. Our instrument is
designed to be reused there, and the attrition analysis
tells that study what to budget for. A second, cheaper
direction is to treat the differential kill as an outcome
in mining studies that already have build access, and to
ask whether it declines before, after, or independently
of the skip ratio — a question about precedence that
requires more than two snapshots.
Finally, we would put the terminological point on
record. “Specification drift” is not an established con-
struct; we define it here as a specialisation of architec-
tural drift Perry and Wolf (1992); Rosik et al. (2010) to
the executable-specification layer. If the term is useful,
it should acquire its own empirical grounding; if it is not,
the underlying phenomenon — a specification artefact
retaining its appearance while losing its discriminating
power — is still real and still measurable, and the name
matters less than the measurement.
8 Threats to validity
We argue each threat explicitly and quantify it where
the data allow. Where a threat could not be quantified,
we say so rather than asserting that it is negligible.
8.1 Survivorship and build attrition
Mechanism. Repositories that build and run are not a
random subset of the frame. Buildable projects plausibly
have simpler dependency graphs, fewer external services
and smaller domains, and these properties may correlate
with both engineering care and oracle quality. The
direction of the resulting bias is not obvious: simpler
domains are easier to test well (upward bias on score),
while projects with sophisticated suites are likelier to
require services, browsers or databases and thus to drop
out (downward bias, because the strongest suites become
unmeasurable).
Quantification. The attrition log records every can-
didate with a categorised reason, and the funnel is re-
ported in full (Section 6). The share of failures at-
tributable to needs_browser and missing_service_
dependency is the measurable footprint of the down-
ward mechanism. We report counts of build_fails,
bdd_fails and no_bdd_execution separately, precisely
so that a reader can reconstruct which mechanism dom-
inates.
Residual. We cannot correct for an unmeasured
common cause such as “engineering care” that may
drive both buildability and oracle quality. Bounds, not
corrections, are available from observational attrition
data, and we do not claim otherwise.
8.2 Mutant representativeness for
business rules
Mechanism. Generic code mutants are a proxy for
changes in business-rule behaviour, not the same thing.
A boundary mutant on a discount threshold plausibly
models a rule change; a mutant in a DTO getter does not.
If the proxy is poor, the outcome measures something
other than business-rule sensitivity.
Mitigation built into the design. Three things limit
the damage. Mutation is restricted to the domain layer,
so infrastructure and glue are excluded by construction.
=== page 14 ===
BDD Suites as Oracles Anonymous submission
The operator set is restricted to decision-logic operators.
And “business-rule relevance” is treated as a separate,
explicitly labelled judgement rather than assumed.
What we could not do. The pre-specified plan called
for two independent human raters to label a random
sample of mutants as business-rule-relevant or not, and
to report Cohen’s κ. No independent human raters were
available for this study, so no κ is reported. We regard
this as a genuine limitation rather than a formality: it
means the strength of the proxy assumption is asserted
on design grounds (domain restriction plus operator
restriction) rather than measured. A study that reports
a κ for this construct would materially strengthen the
evidence, and we recommend it to anyone replicating this
work. We did not substitute an automated labeller for a
human rater, because an automated agreement statistic
would measure the consistency of a heuristic with itself
and would misrepresent it as inter-rater reliability.
8.3 Reliability of the judgement-coded
indicators
Mechanism. D2 (assertion weakness) and D7 (mirror
steps) contain judgement. D2’s weakening patterns are
implemented as explicit syntactic rules, but deciding
whether a rule such as “swallowed failure” applies is a
coding decision; D7’s full definition requires a rater to
inspect the cited domain method.
What is reported. D2 is implemented as an ex-
plicit decision procedure with published rules, and its
confidence is flagged per repository: where more than
a quarter of step definitions cannot be resolved, D2 is
marked low_confidence and excluded from the confir-
matory test. D7 is reported only as an automated census
of its loose conjunction, with no claim of validated pre-
cision.
Residual. Without a second rater we cannot sepa-
rate indicator noise from true variation. Any association
involving D2 or D7 should be read as provisional. This
is stated in the results tables themselves, not only here.
8.4 Coverage confounding
Mechanism. A suite that executes little of the domain
has few opportunities to kill mutants, so a low overall
score can reflect reach rather than discrimination. The
literature is explicit that raw coverage–mutation associ-
ation largely dissolves once restricted to covered code
Jain et al. (2023).
Mitigation. Coverage conditioning is mandatory:
MS all, MS cov and coverage are reported together in
every table, and coverage is a covariate in every model.
The primary outcome is the covered-only score. We go
further and state the interpretation rule verbatim in the
method so that it cannot be quietly abandoned when
results are inconvenient.
8.5 BDD attribution
Mechanism. A suite may be strong for reasons unrelated
to BDD: mature projects have good unit tests, review
culture and stable domains. Attributing a high mutation
score to the Gherkin layer would be a category error.
Mitigation. The differential DK norm is computed
on a common mutant set and reported alongside the
absolute score; BDDshare quantifies how much of the
full suite’s kill the specification layer accounts for. The
design is observational and does not establish that BDD
causes better or worse oracles. We state this in the
introduction, here, and in the conclusion, because it is
the single easiest claim for a reader to over-infer.
8.6 Two-snapshot measurement of drift
Mechanism. Drift is a change over time. Measuring at
HEAD and at one ancestor yields a change between two
snapshots, which is not a trend and cannot distinguish
erosion from a single reorganisation or a bulk edit.
Mitigation. Where mutation is compared across
commits, mutants are matched by file, operator class
andoriginal-linecontenthash, andthechangeisreported
only on the common set, and only when that set covers at
least half of the HEAD mutants; otherwise the repository
is flagged low_overlap. We use the phrase “change
between two snapshots” throughout and never “trend”
or “rate”.
8.7 Public repositories versus industrial
practice
Mechanism. Public repositories differ from industrial
practice in ways that matter: they have no organisation-
specific business rules, no auditors, often no sustained
maintenance, and they are self-selected for public visi-
bility. Conclusions about organisational practice drawn
from them are correspondingly weak.
Position. This is why the paper frames its con-
tribution as an instrument plus a population finding
rather than as a verdict on BDD. The population find-
ing is itself bounded: it describes public topic-tagged
repositories, not all BDD practice.
8.8 Toolchain heterogeneity
Mechanism. PIT, Stryker and mutmut define, name
and count mutants differently, so their scores are not on
a common axis. Kintis et al. found equivalent-mutant
counts of 203, 94, 382 and 43 for the same subjects across
four Java tools, with no tool subsuming the others Kintis
et al. (2018b). Our own harness produced a direct local
instance: for the same domain logic, PIT generated 19
mutants while Stryker generated 43.
Mitigation. Analysis is stratified by language and
tool; no raw pooled correlation is reported as a headline;
where a pooled figure appears it is an average of within-
stratum associations with a cluster bootstrap, and is
labelled as such. Operator sets are stated per tool.
8.9 Validation experiments that were not
performed
Mechanism. The protocol specifies two evaluations that
this study did not carry out: a seeded-drift campaign
(inject known drift into working suites and check that
each indicator flags it, with control injections) and an
inter-rater reliability study for the judgement-coded in-
dicators. Without them, the indicators’ sensitivity and
specificity are unknown, and D2 and D7 in particular
=== page 15 ===
BDD Suites as Oracles Anonymous submission
are unvalidated heuristics.
Disclosure. We state this wherever the in-
dicators are used, and the practitioner protocol
(docs/PROTOCOL.md) carries an explicit evaluation-state
table telling users that seeded-drift validation was not
performed and that no detection rate exists. The
manuscript does not claim that the indicators were vali-
dated. This is the most serious limitation of the artefact:
a measurement instrument whose accuracy has not been
established can still support the decision to investigate,
but it cannot support a quantitative verdict.
8.10 Size-correlated missingness in the
frame
Mechanism. 23 candidate repositories could not be
fetched at all: the archive exceeded the size cap and
the host returned an HTTP 413. These repositories are
recorded as excluded with a distinct reason and are not
counted as analysed.
Why it matters. The missingness is correlated with
size, and project size plausibly correlates with both
engineering maturity and the presence of a domain layer.
If large projects are systematically missing, the eligible
set is biased toward smaller codebases.
Mitigation. The count and the reason are reported
in the funnel rather than folded into “other”, so a reader
can see the magnitude and the mechanism. We did
not raise the cap and retry, because the fetch path is
what a replication would also have to use; a replication
with more generous limits should report a different, and
probably larger, eligible set.
Mechanism. “Specification drift” is not an estab-
lished term, and if a reader interprets it as a received
construct the paper will appear to claim more prior
consensus than exists.
Mitigation. The term is introduced explicitly as a
definition, its established relatives are cited, and Sec-
tion 3 states that the terminological contribution is
minor.
8.11 Environment and reproducibility
Mechanism. Measurements were taken on a single host
without container isolation, in which the Java toolchain
is a single JDK version and mutation tools must be in-
voked outside the process sandbox. A different environ-
ment could produce a different corpus because different
repositories would build.
Mitigation. All tool versions are pinned and
recorded, every measurement carries a UTC timestamp,
harness commit and seed in an append-only run log,
and the screening and analysis scripts are published.
The vulnerability is not hidden: because the corpus is
environment-dependent, we report the full funnel so that
a replication can compare its yield against ours rather
than only its final n.
9 Conclusion
We set out to measure whether public Behaviour-Driven
Development suites still function as oracles, and whether
that capability erodes. We built the instrument, proved
that it can be driven end to end in two ecosystems,
pointed it at a pre-specified frame of public repositories
— and found that the binding constraint is not statistical
but infrastructural.
9.1 Findings
1. The instrument works. Mutation-based oracle mea-
surement driven by the BDD runner itself is fea-
sible in both Java (PIT with the Cucumber-JVM
JUnit-Platform engine) and JavaScript/TypeScript
(Stryker with the official Cucumber runner), with
per-mutant coverage status and per-scenario attri-
bution, which is what the differential-kill construct
requires.
2. The population is thin and skewed. Of 2,552 repos-
itories in the frame, 2,552 were screened, 94 passed
static eligibility, and 0 produced a green, genuinely
executing Gherkin baseline. The eligible set is dom-
inated by BDD tooling — reporting agents, step
libraries, plugins and templates — rather than by
applications with a domain layer. Python is effec-
tively absent from the eligible set, so the planned
three-stratum design could not be realised.
3. The boundary between metrics was confirmed as
load-bearing. Our own harness produced a direct
local instance of cross-tool incomparability: the
same domain logic yielded 19 mutants under PIT
and 43 under Stryker. Any study that pools such
numbers is measuring its tools, not its subject.
4. Where an association could not be estimated, we
say so. The pre-registered confirmatory family is
void under the protocol’s own drop rule, because no
repository was measurable end-to-end. We report
that as not estimated, which is a different statement
from no association, and we do not substitute a
null result for an absent one.
9.2 Contributions revisited
The artefact is a five-step protocol that an organisation
can apply to its own suite, with mandatory coverage
conditioning, mandatory differential attribution, and
explicit interpretation rules that forbid the most com-
mon misreadings. Its practical value does not depend
on the corpus-scale study having succeeded: inside an
organisation, the preconditions it needs — a controllable
build, a domain layer, a green baseline — are achievable,
and the differential kill is then a routine management
metric rather than a research instrument.
The empirical contribution is a quantified account
of why the obvious research design does not work on
the obvious population. We consider this worth publish-
ing precisely because the naive design is attractive and
appears in the literature as an aspiration: a systematic
mapping of 166 BDD papers named the shortage of
specification-quality metrics as a gap, and the natural
response to that gap is to measure quality on public
repositories. Our attrition analysis shows what that
response actually encounters.

9.3 Limitations we could not remove
Three limitations are structural rather than incidental,
and we restate them so they are not lost in the detail
above. No independent human raters were available,
so the judgement-coded indicators carry no inter-rater
reliability statistic and the mutant-relevance proxy is ar-
gued on design grounds rather than measured. No build
isolation was available, so the corpus is environment-
dependent and a different host would likely yield a dif-
ferent n. And the longitudinal component cannot estab-
lish erosion from two snapshots; our strongest available
statement is a change between snapshots on a common
mutant set.
9.4 Future work
The study we could not run is the one worth running: a
corpus of BDD suites drawn from organisations that can
control their build, services and data, measured longi-
tudinally against business-rule change events, with two
independent raters coding the judgement-based indica-
tors so that reliability can be reported. Our instrument
is designed to be reused there, our attrition analysis tells
that study what to budget, and the replication package
contains the harnesses, the screening pipeline and the
analysis code needed to start from where this paper
stopped rather than from the beginning.
